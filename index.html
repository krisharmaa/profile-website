ALGORITHMS · PROJECT NOTES01

Finding the shortest path is only half the story

Building a pathfinding visualizer made me think about what an algorithm trace actually tells a person. A route appears at the end; the interesting part is seeing the search that got there.

Read the note ↗

A shortest-path algorithm can return the right answer and still feel like a black box. When I worked on the map visualizer, I wanted the steps to be visible: which nodes were considered, where the search expanded, and why the route turned where it did.

That changes the experience from “the computer picked this route” to “I can follow how it reached this route.” It also makes differences between approaches easier to notice. Dijkstra’s method explores by accumulated distance; A* uses a heuristic to head toward the destination. Watching both work gives the comparison a shape that a list of names does not.

I’m still learning how to make those explanations clearer without putting too much on screen. That balance—show enough to build understanding, keep the map readable—is the next interaction I’d like to improve.

CAMPUS · PRODUCT THINKING02

A campus portal should answer “is this for me?”

CampusConnect started with a familiar problem: useful events are easy to miss when every announcement is in a different place.

Read the note ↗

A feed can show every event and still leave students doing all the work. The useful question is whether a workshop or deadline fits your course, year, or interests. That became the idea behind CampusConnect’s personalised event view.

Thinking through student and admin roles also helped me see that the same event needs two different paths: one for someone publishing it, another for someone deciding whether to attend. The project is a learning build, and there is more to do before the full database and registration flow are connected end to end.

My takeaway so far: a small amount of relevant context can be more helpful than a large amount of information.

HCI · LEARNING LOG03

What I mean when I say “make it make sense”

I’m beginning to think of interaction design as the small decisions that help someone feel oriented: what can I do, what just happened, and what happens next?

Read the note ↗

I used to think of a good interface mostly in terms of how it looked. I still care about that, but the projects I enjoy most make me ask a different question: can someone tell what the system is doing without having to guess?

In a visualizer, that might mean showing progress as the search unfolds. In a campus portal, it might mean making the event details and audience clear before someone opens another page. These are ordinary moments, but they shape whether a tool feels easy to trust.

I don’t have a neat formula for this yet. I’m learning by building, noticing where I get confused myself, and trying to make the next version a little clearer.

A living notebook · I’ll add more as I build and learn.
